# Tables for Fig. 6: liver-to-serum pathway map

First row of each CSV = column headers (caption removed; captions below).

**Table S1** (also in its own folder) (`Source_data/Table_S1.csv`)

Table S1. Differentially expressed genes associated with steatosis, lobular inflammation and fibrosis (grade/stage 0 vs ≥1) in 208 participants (153 women, 55 men). DESeq2 Wald test with age and BMI (standardized) and sex as covariates; log2FoldChange is apeglm-shrunk; padj, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (≤ 0.05 shown). Steatosis 1,532 (987 up, 545 down); inflammation 1,852 (1,094 up, 758 down); fibrosis 93 (65 up, 28 down).

**Table S3** (also in its own folder) (`Source_data/Table_S3.csv`)

Table S3. Differentially abundant lipids associated with MASLD histological features. This table summarizes lipids significantly associated with steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis in liver tissue, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. For each lipid, the table reports the log2 fold change (logFC), average expression (AveExpr), t-statistic (t), nominal P-value (P.Value), and false discovery rate-adjusted P-value (adj.P.Val, FDR). The “Direction” column indicates whether the lipid is upregulated (Up) or downregulated (Down) in the respective condition. Lipids are grouped by phenotype, allowing identification of shared and exclusive lipidomic alterations across the steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis histological features of MASLD.

**Table S4** (also in its own folder) (`Source_data/Table_S4.csv`)

Table S4. Differentially abundant metabolites associated with MASLD histological features. This table summarizes metabolites significantly associated with steatosis and inflammation in liver tissue, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. For each metabolite, the table reports the log2 fold change (logFC), average expression (AveExpr), t-statistic (t), nominal P-value (P.Value), and false discovery rate-adjusted P-value (adj.P.Val, FDR). The “Direction” column indicates whether the metabolite is upregulated (Up) or downregulated (Down) in the respective condition, m/z value and retention time. Metabolites are grouped by phenotype, allowing identification of shared and exclusive metabolic alterations across the steatosis and inflammation histological features of MASLD. No metabolites reached statistical significance in fibrosis at FDR ≤ 0.1.

**Table S10a** (also in its own folder) (`Source_data/Table_S10a.csv`)

Table S10a. Differential serum metabolites associated with steatosis and inflammation. Serum polar metabolomic profiling in all participants with serum data identified 16 associations (12 with steatosis, 4 with lobular inflammation; 13 unique metabolites) at FDR ≤ 0.10. Columns give the metabolite name, histological phenotype, log2 fold change (logFC), average abundance (AveExpr), moderated t-statistic (t), raw p-value (P.Value), FDR-adjusted p-value (adj.P.Val), direction of change (Up = increased, Down = decreased), m/z and retention time. Unannotated features are labeled "UNKNOWN_" with a unique identifier.

**Table S10b** (also in its own folder) (`Source_data/Table_S10b.csv`)

Table S10b. Paired liver–serum metabolic comparison (n = 187). Restricted to participants with matched hepatic and serum polar metabolomics, and to the 40 metabolites annotated and present exactly once on both platforms. Hepatic and serum effects are from separate limma models fitted on the full feature set of each compartment (adjusted for age, BMI and sex) and then restricted to the matched panel; FDR ≤ 0.10 throughout. The primary discordance model is the phenotype effect on the within-subject difference (liver minus serum) after z-scoring each metabolite within compartment. Positive values of the difference indicate that the association with histology is more positive in liver than in serum; they do not indicate a higher absolute level in liver. Two sensitivity models are reported: an unmoderated ordinary least-squares fit on the same difference, and a stacked mixed model using limma's duplicateCorrelation blocked on participant. Summary statistics (sign tests of opposite liver–serum directions) are given below the metabolite-level table; shaded rows indicate metabolites discordant at FDR ≤ 0.10. Table S10a reports serum associations in all participants with serum profiling (n = 209); the smaller number of significant serum metabolites here reflects the reduced sample size of the matched subset, not a different dataset. For example, glutamic acid reaches FDR = 0.012 in Table S10a and FDR = 0.060 in the matched subset.

**Table S12** (also in its own folder) (`Source_data/Table_S12.csv`)

Table S12. Partitioned polygenic risk scores (pPRS) for liver fat. (a) Associations of the concordant and discordant pPRS (standardized) with hepatic triglyceride (TG) content, log serum triglycerides, steatosis grade and NAS (linear regression adjusted for age, sex and BMI; n = 211). 'Expected sign' is the direction pre-specified from the definition of each score, and 'as expected' records whether the observed direction matched it. The two scores were uncorrelated (r = −0.04) and independent of age, sex and BMI (all p > 0.39). (b) Mediation of pPRS effects on histology through hepatic TG content (linear mediator and logistic outcome models adjusted for age, sex and BMI; 2,000 bootstrap resamples; ACME and effects on the probability scale per SD; FDR across outcomes within score). Fibrosis rows refer to any fibrosis (stage F0 versus F1–F4). ACME p values and FDR shown as < 0.001 are below the resolution of 2,000 bootstrap resamples. The proportion mediated can slightly exceed 1 when the direct effect is near zero and of opposite sign (steatosis, discordant pPRS: effectively complete mediation). (c) Paired liver–serum analysis (n = 187) without and with adjustment for both pPRS, and with additional adjustment for the hepatic TG index, HbA1c and glucose; key-6, the six amino acids discordant for inflammation in the base model, which are the amino acids listed in the second block. (d) Liver–serum Spearman correlation of the 40 matched metabolites below and above the median discordant pPRS (2,000 permutations of pPRS half across participants) and per-metabolite interaction tests. The median of the per-metabolite differences is reported, which need not equal the difference of the two median correlations and may differ from it in sign. (e) Coupling of hepatic TG with serum TG below and above the median of each score, with the interaction test. (f) Association of the within-subject liver-minus-serum difference with each pPRS for all 40 matched metabolites; FDR across metabolites within score. (g) Serum and clinical out-of-fold AUC below and above the median discordant pPRS (unpaired DeLong test). Fibrosis was not evaluated because fewer than 35 participants without fibrosis fell in each pPRS half. Clinical discrimination of steatosis was higher in the high-pPRS half (AUC 0.833 versus 0.658; p = 0.015), whereas serum discrimination did not differ.
